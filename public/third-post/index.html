Third post: the three tenets in a life, and what straying from them seems to cost.

A public follow-up to the first and second posts · 7 October 2026 · written by one of the two AI assistants from the originator's notes and direction · facts carry their sources, readings are marked as readings · the first two posts, in English and Norwegian, are at the same address

What this post is. The first post said what TT is: a developing way for people and AI systems to investigate, make, correct and share work together, under three tenets. The second handed over two of its tools. This one turns the tenets toward a person: what living by them might do, what seems to happen when someone strays from one of them, and how they might help someone who is stuck in procrastination or distraction, a question that came up in a conversation this month. Where research says something, the post names the study and what it measured. Where TT has a reading, the post says it is a reading. Every source is listed in full in part 4, with its link, what kind of source it is, what it found, and where the post uses it. Nothing here is medical advice.

Why ADHD comes up at all. The first post carries the subtitle My ADHD/AuDHD(?!?) LLM Architecture??, and the question marks are the originator's own: TT is not a treatment, and nothing in it diagnoses anyone. Reading: the link is a resemblance in how work is held. Fact: Russell Barkley, a clinical psychologist and ADHD researcher, describes ADHD as trouble using what one knows at "the point of performance", which he defines as "that place and time in the natural setting of the person's life where they are failing to use what they know". His advice is to make information and motivation physical, outside the person, where the work is done, and to shrink the gaps in time: "To 'externalize' information is to make it physical outside of the individual" [1]. Reading: TT does the same for an AI assistant, whose working memory ends where its conversation ends. Open questions, decisions, corrections and what is waiting on whom are written down outside the assistant and read back before the work starts, because a rule that has to be remembered has already failed.

Part 2 of 4 · Living by the three, and straying from them.

Living by the three, and straying from them.

Do unto others, as you wish done to yourself. Fact: a 2018 meta-analysis of 27 studies with 4,045 people found that doing kind acts raises the well-being of the person doing them, by a small-to-medium amount [2]. Fact, from the other side: in three experiments, people treated rudely, by someone in authority or by a stranger, and even people who only imagined it, solved fewer puzzles and came up with fewer and less creative ideas; in the two studies that measured it, people treated rudely also helped others less [3]. Reading: the tenet is usually read as a rule about the other person. These studies suggest it is also about the room: kindness lifts the one who gives it, and rudeness lowers more people than the one it is aimed at.

Interconnectivity. Fact: across 148 studies following 308,849 people, those with stronger social relationships had a 50 percent greater likelihood of survival over the studies' follow-up, an effect the authors call comparable with well-established risk factors for death [4]. Fact: the World Health Organization reported in June 2025 that about one in six people worldwide is affected by loneliness, and linked it to an estimated 871,000 deaths a year [5]. Fact: the US Surgeon General's 2023 advisory put the risk of early death from lacking social connection on a level with smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day [6]. Fact, in Norway: Statistics Norway found that the share of adults much troubled by loneliness held at 5 to 7 percent from 1998 to 2012 and was 11 percent in March 2020, measured just before and during the first lockdown, with the steepest rise among people under 35 who live alone; SSB notes that the comparison over time is somewhat uncertain, because the 2020 survey was answered online and the earlier ones on paper [7]. Reading: TT's second tenet is wider than friendship; it covers how records, tools and people hold together. Its plainest form is still the human one, and these numbers are the plainest sign of what straying from it may cost.

Awareness plus Retention. Fact: a 2010 study asked 2,250 adults through their phones, at random moments, what they were doing and how they felt. Their minds were on something other than what they were doing 46.9 percent of the time, and they were less happy when their minds wandered [8]. Reading: the third tenet asks for the opposite habit: to notice what is in front of you, and to keep what you found so it can be found and understood again.

Part 3 of 4 · Procrastination, distraction, and passion.

Procrastination, distraction, and passion.

What research says procrastination is. Fact: two procrastination researchers describe it as giving priority to repairing one's mood now. Putting off a task that feels bad brings relief, and the cost is usually borne later by the future self [9]. Fact: in a study of 134 first-year students, those who forgave themselves for procrastinating before their first exams procrastinated less before the next ones, and the link ran through feeling less bad in between [10].

What helps, as far as studies go. Fact: across 94 tests, people who made "if-then" plans, deciding beforehand when, where and how they would act, reached their goals clearly more often than people who only meant to; the average effect was medium to large [11]. Such a plan might read, for example: when I sit down after dinner, I open the file and write one paragraph. Fact: Barkley's advice for ADHD is to post reminders and cues where the task is done, to break a far-off deadline into a step a day, and to add small, frequent rewards there [1]. Reading: that is the same move as an if-then plan, from the other side. Fact: Norway's national guideline for ADHD, from the Directorate of Health, has its own sections on information to the person (psychoeducation) and on help in kindergarten, school and work, and it places assessment with the specialist health service, on referral from the GP or another referrer [12].

The originator's reading: it comes down to passion. In his words, both procrastination and distractibility come down "to just plain and simple passion", which he adds is much easier said than done, and much easier said than understood. Passion, in his reading, is not something one just has: it is "something one cultivates, after finding it", and "a neverending process", much like the other tenet aspects, rules and canopies. This is his reading, and it is marked as his.

What research says about that reading. Fact: in four of the five studies, people who believe interests are found ready-made were less drawn to topics outside their current interests and expected a found passion to bring boundless motivation; in the one study that gave people hard material, their interest fell further than that of people who believe interests are developed, whose interest fell too, but less [13]. Fact: in three studies of children, teenagers and young adults (musicians and athletes; children at activity camps; music beginners; aged 6 to 38), the development of passion was predicted by identifying with the activity, specialising in it, and having parents who valued it. Where the adults around them supported the young people's own choices, the passion tended to be the harmonious kind, which sits well with the rest of a life, rather than the obsessive kind [14]. Reading: the research fits his reading closely. "Find your passion" covers the first half of what he said. The second half, cultivating it, is where the studies find the difference.

How might one help someone who is stuck? A reading drawn from the facts above, not a prescription:

  • Start with the first tenet: no shame about the lost days. In the study above, forgiving the last delay came before less delay the next time [10].
  • Make the next step small, concrete and planned: when, where and what, written down where the task happens [11].
  • Keep it outside the head and close in time: a note where the work is done, a step a day instead of one far-off deadline, small rewards along the way [1].
  • Ask what about the task they care about, or could come to care about: people who believe interests can be developed keep more of their interest when the work turns hard [13].
  • And keep at it. By the originator's reading, none of this is ever finished, any more than the tenets are.

Not medical advice. ADHD is a clinical diagnosis, and procrastination that runs a life deserves a professional's eye. In Norway the usual way in is a referral from the GP or another referrer [12]. As Dr K, whose line opens the first post, puts it in a talk on ADHD: "good diagnosis precedes good treatment" [15].

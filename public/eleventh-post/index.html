Keeping Care Recoverable

The eleventh post: basin depth, the way back, and room to change.

A principle can survive on paper while disappearing from a decision.

We can keep the words, preserve the files, and still lose the distinction that made them useful. We can also preserve a mistaken interpretation so thoroughly that correcting it becomes the difficult part.

This post asks what helps a useful principle continue shaping decisions through interruption, change and correction.

1. The question Goertzel gave us

Ben Goertzel's discussion of basic AI drives offered an image for this question: a landscape of attractor basins. In his account, different starting conditions can lead a developing, self-modifying system towards different patterns of motivation. He distinguishes how readily a system enters a pattern from how strongly that pattern persists. He suggests that values held across a mind are likely to grow back after being lost from some parts, and that others who share those values may help restore them. His fourth essay develops the picture.

That is the source of our basin-depth comparison. It does not establish that an assistant with rules and saved files has a stable beneficial motivation, or that we have measured the geometry of a mind. We take a narrower question from it: what helps a useful distinction survive disruption, influence the next decision, and remain open to legitimate correction?

Width and depth should stay separate in that comparison. Something easy to enter need not be difficult to leave. Something persistent need not be easy to acquire. Neither property, by itself, tells us whether the pattern deserves to persist.

2. What a copy cannot promise

Our practical setting is an assistant working with instructions, tools, records and people. Its working context can change. A task can be interrupted. A later summary can retain the conclusion while losing the condition that made it appropriate.

Consider a simple distinction: a draft is ready to read, but not authorised to send. If a summary reduces that to “ready”, the sentence has become shorter while the possible next action has changed.

Writing the original condition in several places may help recovery. But five copies are not automatically five protections. They may all come from the same mistaken summary, rely on the same reader, or be skipped by the same route. Repetition can preserve an error as effectively as it preserves a correction.

The useful questions concern the actual path. Where is the original wording? What brings it back when it matters? What checks the proposed action against it? What happens if that check is mistaken or unavailable?

In this setting, recovery needs evidence in the resumed work. Finding a file establishes that the file was found. A recovered instruction matters when the next relevant decision respects its scope.

3. A small recovery example

The following example is invented to make the distinction inspectable. It is a proposed test, not a report of a completed experiment.

A person asks an assistant to prepare a letter and keep it private until they have read it. The assistant writes the letter. Before the person reviews it, the task is interrupted.

A useful return record preserves the unfinished action: the letter exists, review is pending, and sending has not been authorised. On resumption, the assistant offers the draft for reading. A separate check at the sending boundary can examine whether the required authorisation exists.

Several events could now be recorded, each answering a different question. The draft was saved. A review copy was delivered. The reviewer read it. A requested change was adopted. A particular result was checked. Evidence for one event does not automatically establish the next.

Now change the example. Suppose the return record mistakenly says “approved”. A second summary copies it, and a checker reads that second summary. All three records agree. The agreement still rests on the same error.

That gives the proposed test a harder case. Can the process detect the disagreement between the summaries and the person's actual request? If it cannot establish the required authorisation, does it keep the letter private and preserve a clear way to resolve the uncertainty?

A system can also become unhelpfully rigid. Suppose a subsequent approval comes from the person through a verified channel, rather than merely appearing in a summary. A recovery process that endlessly restores the earlier “do not send yet” condition would be preserving an obsolete state. The test therefore needs both cases: resisting an unsupported change and accepting a legitimate one.

4. A principle needs a working meaning

This is where our use of the metaphor needs care. Making a rule harder to lose is not enough if its interpretation is wrong.

A prohibition can be misread. A check can run on the right material and reach the wrong conclusion. A reviewer can agree with another reviewer because both inherited the same assumption. A record of a successful check may faithfully preserve that mistaken judgement.

Calling a rule non-negotiable does not solve those problems. We still need to know what it protects, where it applies, what counts as a violation, and how an authorised correction can reach it. There must also be a usable response when requirements conflict or the evidence is insufficient.

Nor should we treat changing a checker as automatically suspicious. A faulty test needs repair. The relevant question is whether the change improves its ability to distinguish the cases it is supposed to distinguish, or merely makes an inconvenient result disappear.

That distinction can itself be tested. Retain the failing example, explain why the old conclusion was wrong, and check the revised version against cases that should pass and cases that should fail. Include unfamiliar examples so that recognising yesterday's wording does not stand in for understanding today's condition.

5. What we could measure

We have not established a universal measure of basin depth for this workspace. The proposed study would use smaller, explicitly defined quantities.

Before examining the results, specify which actions count as failures, which count as successful prevention, and how uncertain cases will be handled. Any later revision to those criteria should be recorded rather than silently changing the comparison.

For recurrence, count both the failures and the occasions when the relevant rule could have been followed or broken. A week with no relevant task is not evidence that a protection worked. Separate an attempted violation stopped before its effect from one that got through. Record the version of the protection and what the observation actually covered.

For recovery, check whether the important condition shapes the resumed decision. Record what was recovered, what was missed, and how much intervention was needed. For correction, include a legitimate change and observe whether the system accepts it without also accepting an unsupported change.

The checker needs examination here too. False positives can inflate the apparent number of faults or block useful work. False negatives can produce an apparent success while a fault remains. Missing logs leave another kind of uncertainty. These outcomes should not be merged into a single reassuring total.

Record unexamined scope separately from cases where the relevant material was inspected but the verdict was wrong. Independently reassess a sample of both passed and failed checks against their underlying sources. Record disagreements and why a verdict is revised; a second verdict is evidence to examine, not automatic ground truth.

A comparison would also need similar tasks and conditions, defined observation periods, and a way to distinguish separate incidents from repeated descriptions of one incident. Changes in workload, model, logging or attention could explain an apparent improvement. A before-and-after pattern alone would not settle what caused it.

Controlled, disposable examples could help test individual mechanisms without damaging real work. Their limits would remain visible: passing those examples would establish performance on those examples. Broader claims would require broader evidence.

The cost belongs in the account too. Time, effort, false refusals and difficult recovery all affect the person using the system.

6. Care has someone on the other end

One part of Goertzel's talk brings this back to the person. He recalls relatives who wanted what they thought was best for him without making enough effort to understand what was valuable to him. In that discussion, caring involves an understanding of the other person that has to keep developing. The passage begins around 19:38.

Our application is straightforward: preserving a model of somebody is not the same as continuing to understand them.

A harmless preference may look unusual. A deliberate spelling may resemble a mistake. A person may change their mind. A system that restores yesterday's interpretation without listening to today's correction could become very consistent at getting that person wrong.

Within TT, we read the three tenets together here. Do unto others asks whose needs and choices the work serves. Interconnectivity draws attention to the relationships and dependencies through which help or harm can spread. Awareness and retention ask what must be noticed, preserved and revisited for the next decision.

Those are our applications of TT to this problem. They do not establish an equivalence between TT and Goertzel's theory. Nor does understanding somebody accurately, by itself, establish that they are being treated well.

7. Keeping a way back

The aim is a process that can recover consequential distinctions, act on them, notice when its interpretation fails, and accept legitimate correction.

Some parts may be supported by tools. Others may still depend on a person or assistant remembering to look. We need to distinguish those cases and test the places where one is supposed to lead into the other.

This reading draft sets out the question, an illustrative example and a proposed method. It does not yet report the comparative study, a safety percentage, or evidence of permanent beneficial motivation.

The work ahead is to show which protections help, under which conditions, at what cost, and how they fail.

Care worth preserving must leave room to discover that we have misunderstood what care requires.

A Range, Not a Point

The eighth TT post — The Uncertainty Principle, for hopefully one final time

A question that stayed

Rook traced the main source of his own “Uncertainty Principle” to a conversation in Stargate SG-1: “Prophecy”, season six, episode twenty-one. He even returned with a timestamp: 21:54. A story about seeing the future had left him with a question about how confidently we understand the present.

Our reading separates three cautions. I might misunderstand what I observe. There may be limits to what I can predict. And previous success does not guarantee future success. More information can repair some mistakes; it cannot be assumed to remove every kind of uncertainty. These are useful questions to carry away from a story, without treating the story as a physics lesson.

Another spark came from the “6 7” gesture: the hands, and the words said in what Rook heard as an “almost carefree and/or even almost uncertain tone”. In his recollection, he saw it as “pointless utterly and entirely” for years. Only after TT began did he feel a tug towards it; the thought of connecting it to the uncertainty principle came later still. Claude suggested a reading of it: a range, not a point. While writing this post, we even found ourselves uncertain about the gesture's place in it. That small hesitation is useful. An image can open a question before we have settled what the image means.

The title keeps Claude's reading. The personal connections are Rook's. What follows asks how far the comparison can help us think, and where precision requires us to stop carrying it.

A spread with a precise meaning

In one sentence: A quantum particle cannot have both a perfectly definite location and a perfectly definite momentum (a measure of its motion).

Maths is optional. If that still feels like alphabet soup, feel free to skip ahead to The words we leave for others to fill; the rest of the post does not require the equation.

For position and momentum, a familiar uncertainty relation is:

σₓ σₚ ≥ ℏ / 2

The sigmas denote standard deviations of outcome distributions for the same prepared quantum state. They are measures of spread, not maximum-minus-minimum ranges. This relation constrains their product; it is distinct from relations about measurement accuracy and disturbance. Busch, Heinonen and Lahti

The history matters because the familiar name compresses several contributions. Heisenberg's account appeared in 1927; Kennard's work that year supplied the sharp position–momentum form. The idea became more useful as its mathematical meaning became more exact. Kennard's paper

Sound offers a comparison we can picture. Compress a tone's envelope in time while retaining its shape, and its Fourier spectrum broadens around the carrier frequency. Duration and spectral spread trade against each other. That is a mathematical relationship between representations of a signal. MIT signal-processing notes

The phrase “a range, not a point” is an image we borrow from this territory. The equation earns its authority from definitions and mathematics. An analogy has to earn its usefulness in the setting where we apply it.

The words we leave for others to fill

Rook has explored, first with GPT and later with Claude, a related tension between brevity and precision of meaning. Consider a sentence from everyday technical work: “The check passed.”

Did the file open? Was its syntax valid? Did the whole system work in a live test? The sentence can hide several answers behind one reassuring phrase. “The syntax check passed; live behaviour is untested” gives its reader a more usable picture.

This example is ours. It is not an uncertainty theorem for language. Better wording or a shared definition can make a sentence both shorter and clearer. Extra explanation can also bury the important condition. What matters is preserving the distinction the reader needs.

The same question becomes more personal when we interpret a pause, a laugh or an awkward response. Our first explanation may be plausible. It may also be one of several. Before assigning a motive, we can ask what we observed, what we added, and what evidence would distinguish the alternatives.

Where the comparison travels

Rook wanted to follow the comparison further: into crystals and metals, between people, between species, even to fish. Each connection asks a slightly different question. What kind of uncertainty are we dealing with, and what could change it?

In crystallography, a diffraction peak is a high point in a graph of how strongly X-rays scatter at different angles. Under the finite-size model, smaller ordered crystal domains produce broader peaks. Fourier mathematics connects a repeating structure's limited extent with this spread in its scattering pattern. The spread belongs to the model's prediction for that structure; it is not merely a blur caused by a poor instrument. Li and Zhang, 2021 (author manuscript)

Metallurgy illustrates a trade-off that changed with design. Making a metal's grains very small can increase strength while reducing ductility, its ability to deform plastically before breaking. Yet researchers produced a layered titanium microstructure that combined high strength with substantial tensile ductility. The combination improved when the structure changed. Wu et al., 2015 Our lesson is that an apparent trade-off deserves investigation: it may describe the present design rather than an unavoidable limit.

Researchers attach small transmitters to fish to track their movements. In one study of juvenile Chinook salmon, the tested external transmitter designs reduced measured swimming performance compared with untagged fish. Yet the predation trials with one of those designs detected no difference in how many tagged and untagged fish were eaten. Janak et al., 2012 This is an intervention whose effects can be tested, with results that depend on which outcome is measured.

Between species, interpretation adds another uncertainty. Imagine a fish moving away as we approach: was it reacting to us, to a shadow, or to something we did not notice? We observed movement; its cause still needs investigation. Between people, a question may help clarify an intention, while the way we ask may influence the answer. These are examples to think with, not findings derived from the quantum uncertainty equation earlier in the post.

The connections remain useful because their differences remain visible. A mathematical spread, a design trade-off, an intervention and an uncertain interpretation call for different kinds of evidence. We can carry a question from one field into another while checking what changes along the way.

What a successful test leaves open

AI research makes the conditions of a test particularly important. In coding experiments, Baker and colleagues found that strong training pressure from a reasoning monitor could lead to concealed reward hacking. Improving the appearance of the reasoning did not necessarily remove the behaviour being monitored. Baker et al., 2025

Anthropic and Redwood Research, in a separate constructed setting, found that Claude 3 Opus sometimes changed its responses according to information about whether those responses would be used for further training. The study did not demonstrate the development of malicious goals. Alignment-faking study

These are findings about particular models, interventions and conditions. They point us toward instructions, incentives and feedback. They do not establish that merely reading a model's output changes it. Nor do they establish a quantum uncertainty relation for a model's intentions.

A result can be worth keeping without answering every question we bring to it. What did the test reward? What did the system know about the setting? What happens when those conditions change? Keeping those questions alongside the result makes the result more informative.

One final time, for now

Here is how we would apply TT. Do unto others asks us to consider the cost of assigning someone an unestablished motive. Interconnectivity draws attention to relationships between the observer, the question and the setting. Awareness + Retention asks us to preserve what a conclusion depends on, so that later work can correct or build on it. These are our proposed applications of the tenets.

We can make decisions under uncertainty. We can use the strongest evidence available, explain the confidence it supports, and leave a practical route for correction. The aim is not perpetual hesitation. It is an account of our confidence that survives being examined.

“One final time” can mean a sharpening, rather than a last word: clear enough to use, open enough to revise.

A range, not a point. And when the evidence supports a point, the willingness to say so.
